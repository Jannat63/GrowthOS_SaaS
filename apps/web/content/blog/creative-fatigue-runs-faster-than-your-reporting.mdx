There is a specific way Meta budget gets wasted that has nothing to do with targeting, bidding, or landing pages. It is a timing mismatch.

Creative fatigue — the point where an audience has seen an ad enough times that response falls off — arrives within roughly 72 hours for many audience sizes at meaningful spend. The cadence most teams use to catch it is a weekly review. So a creative turns on Wednesday, and gets looked at the following Monday, having spent five days past the point where it was working.

Nobody is being careless. The review happens on schedule. The schedule is just slower than the thing it is reviewing.

## Fatigue does not announce itself in ROAS

The reason it goes unnoticed for so long is that the metric people watch is the last one to move.

The sequence usually runs:

1. **Click-through rate falls first.** Same audience, same creative, less reason to stop scrolling. This is the earliest honest signal.
2. **Frequency climbs.** The delivery system, still trying to hit its target, shows the ad to the same people more often.
3. **CPM drifts up.** You are now bidding into an audience that responds less.
4. **Cost per acquisition rises.**
5. **ROAS finally drops** — days after step one, and only once the conversion window catches up.

If ROAS is your trigger, you are acting on step five. By then you have paid for the whole decline. The teams that stay ahead of this watch step one and step two, and treat a CTR decline against the creative's own first-week baseline as the alarm, not a ROAS threshold.

That baseline matters. Absolute CTR benchmarks are close to useless across accounts — a 1.1% CTR is strong in some categories and weak in others. What is diagnostic is a creative's current CTR against what that same creative did in its first 72 hours, on the same audience.

## The refresh problem is a content problem

Knowing when to rotate is the easy half. The hard half is having something to rotate to.

Meta's delivery now rewards volume of distinct creative — a working ad set can consume 15 to 25 variants a week. Most teams cannot brief, produce, and ship at that rate, so what actually happens is minor variations on the same concept: new colour, new crop, same idea. Audiences fatigue on the idea, not the crop. Variations of a tired concept fatigue almost immediately.

So the question is where new *concepts* come from, at a rate that matches the burn.

## Where the next hook is already sitting

Two sources are usually available and usually ignored.

**Your organic content that ranks.** A page holding a top-three position has passed a test: enough people wanted that specific angle that search rewarded it. That is validated demand for a topic, and it is a reasonable seed for a top-of-funnel concept — the audience for the page and the audience for the ad are frequently the same people at a different moment.

**Your winning hooks, running the other way.** A hook that beats 3% CTR on Meta has demonstrated something more specific than "people liked it." It found an angle on the product that made a stranger stop. That is a headline, a page title, and a content brief. Creative testing is audience research that most teams throw away after the campaign ends.

Both directions are worth wiring, and both are unglamorous operational work rather than a clever idea: watch CTR against a per-creative baseline, keep a queue of concepts sourced from what is already working elsewhere, and rotate before the ROAS line confirms what the CTR line said on Tuesday.

## What to change on Monday

Three things, none of which need new tooling:

- **Set the alarm on CTR decline against the creative's own baseline**, not on a ROAS floor. Review it daily; it takes two minutes.
- **Track days-live per creative** and treat anything past two weeks in a hot audience as suspect by default.
- **Keep a briefed queue, not a wish list.** The rotation only helps if the replacement exists when the alarm fires.

The budget lost to this is not dramatic on any single day. It is a few percent of spend, continuously, in the gap between when a creative stops working and when your reporting cadence admits it.
