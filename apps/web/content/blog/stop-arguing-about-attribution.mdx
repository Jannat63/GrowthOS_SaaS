Add up what your ad platforms claim they generated. Compare it to what your store actually took. The first number is bigger — often by a lot.

This is not fraud, and it is usually not a tracking bug. It is arithmetic. Google counts a conversion it can see. Meta counts a conversion it can see, on a wider window that includes people who merely *viewed* an ad. Your analytics counts a session that it could stitch together. One purchase, touched by three channels, gets counted by all three. Everyone reports honestly within their own frame, and the frames overlap.

The usual response is to try to arbitrate — pick a model, argue about windows, build a spreadsheet that reconciles it. That work rarely converges, because there is no measurement that settles it. The overlap is real, and the platforms have no way and no incentive to see past their own edge.

## The number that cannot be inflated

Blended MER — total revenue divided by total marketing spend — is almost aggressively unsophisticated. It attributes nothing. It does not know which ad did what. It cannot tell you where to move budget on its own.

That is the point. Because it never assigns credit, there is nothing to double-count. Every dollar out, every dollar in, one ratio:

```
Blended MER = total revenue ÷ total marketing spend
```

It is the one figure in your reporting that a platform cannot move by widening a window. When it goes up, marketing got more efficient. When it goes down, it didn't. Finance already thinks this way, which is a large part of why finance and marketing so often disagree about whether a quarter went well.

## Its honest limitations

Anyone selling MER as a complete answer is overselling it. Three real limits:

**It lags.** Longer consideration cycles mean this month's spend shows up in a later month's revenue. MER is a trend instrument, read over 30, 60, 90 days. Reading it week to week mostly produces noise.

**It moves for non-marketing reasons.** A pricing change, a stockout, a seasonal peak, a PR moment — all move MER without anything in the ad account changing. Anomalies need context before they need action.

**It cannot allocate budget by itself.** MER tells you the machine got more or less efficient. It does not tell you which lever did it. For that you still need per-channel attribution — you just need it demoted from arbiter to diagnostic.

## The structure that works

Use MER as the scoreboard and attribution models as the diagnostics underneath it.

Blended MER answers *are we getting more efficient?* — one number, no arguments, the one that goes in the board deck.

Underneath it, run several attribution models side by side rather than choosing one. Last-click, first-click, linear, time-decay, position-based. The value is not in picking a winner; it is in the disagreement between them. A channel that looks strong under last-click and weak under first-click is closing demand someone else created. A channel that inverts that is creating demand it never gets credit for closing. That gap is the actual insight, and choosing a single model destroys it.

Then hold spend decisions to the scoreboard. Shift budget based on the models, and check whether blended MER improved. If it did not, the model was telling you a story about credit, not about incremental revenue.

## Why this ends the argument

The reason attribution debates go on forever is that both sides are arguing from numbers that are internally consistent and mutually irreconcilable. There is no evidence either can produce that would settle it, so the meeting repeats monthly.

One number that nobody can inflate ends that. It is not more accurate than the models — in a sense it knows far less. But it is unarguable, and unarguable turns out to be worth more in a meeting than sophisticated.

Start the report with it. Put the five models underneath, where their disagreement can be useful instead of disruptive. Then spend the recovered hour on something that changes the number rather than on explaining it.
